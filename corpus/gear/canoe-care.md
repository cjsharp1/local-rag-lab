# Canoe Care: Carrying, Loading, and Looking After Your Rental

A canoe that is handled well will carry you through a week in the Kettle Basin Canoe Area without a second thought. A canoe that is dragged, dropped, or overloaded can end a trip early. This guide covers how our boats are built, how to carry and load them, and what to do at the end of each day. It applies to every canoe we rent: the Northline 17 Kevlar, Northline 17 Aluminum, Wayfarer 18.5, and Kestrel 15.

## Know your hull

### Kevlar: light, but brittle on rock

Our Kevlar canoes are light because the hull is a thin, stiff layup rather than heavy metal. That stiffness is also the weakness: Kevlar is brittle when it strikes rock. A sharp hit on a submerged boulder, or dropping the boat onto a rocky landing, can crack or puncture the hull in a way that an aluminum canoe would simply dent. Treat a Kevlar boat gently in shallow water, get out before the hull grounds, and carry it over any rough ground instead of sliding it.

### Aluminum: tough, but it dents

The Northline 17 Aluminum is nearly indestructible, but it is not dent-proof. A hard impact can push in the hull, and a bad one can bend a rib. Aluminum also slides poorly over rock and grabs on it, so the same habits that protect Kevlar apply here too.

### Scratches are normal

Every boat in our fleet has scratches and scuffs from earlier trips. That is normal wear and we don't charge for it. What we look for at return is cracks, punctures, gouges, dents, and broken seats or yokes. Our damage and loss policy lists how those are handled.

## Lift, don't drag

The single most important habit: never drag a canoe over rocks or the landing. Lift it clear, even if you are only moving it a few feet. Dragging grinds the bottom of the hull at the same spot every time, and on a rocky landing it takes only one hidden edge to open a Kevlar layup.

At every landing:

1. Paddle in slowly and stop before the hull touches bottom.
2. The bow paddler steps out into the shallows and steadies the boat.
3. Unload packs while the canoe is still floating.
4. Both paddlers lift the empty boat and carry it to the trail or campsite.

When you launch, reverse the order: carry the empty boat into water deep enough to float it, then load.

## Carrying a canoe

### Two-person carry

For short distances, such as from the car rack to the water or across a campsite, one paddler takes each end by the carry handles and walks the boat at waist height. Keep it level and watch your footing.

### Solo portage on the yoke

On portages, one person carries the canoe overturned on their shoulders using the center yoke. Every rental comes with yoke pads; strap them on before the first portage.

To get the canoe up:

1. Stand at the center of the boat, facing it, with the canoe upright on the ground.
2. Grip the yoke near the center and roll the boat up onto your thighs.
3. Reach across to the far gunwale, then rotate the boat up and over your head in one smooth motion, setting the yoke pads on your shoulders.
4. Hold the gunwales or thwarts ahead of you to keep the bow slightly raised so you can see the trail.

A partner can help by lifting the bow while you get under the yoke. Coming down is the same in reverse; set the boat down, never drop it. The 44 lb Northline 17 Kevlar and 32 lb Kestrel 15 are comfortable solo carries for most adults. The 72 lb Northline 17 Aluminum is a solo carry only for strong portagers; otherwise carry it two-person.

### Resting on the trail

On long portages, rest the bow in the fork of a tree or on a rock ledge at shoulder height instead of setting the whole boat down. That saves you the lift to get going again and keeps the hull off the ground.

## Loading the canoe

A well-loaded canoe is stable and easy to steer. A badly loaded one wallows, catches wind, and wears you out.

- **Load while floating.** Never load a canoe resting on the shore; the weight presses the hull into the rocks.
- **Heavy items low and centered.** The food barrel and the heaviest portage pack go in the middle, on the floor, between the paddlers.
- **Trim it level.** Side to side, the boat should sit flat. Front to back, it should be level or very slightly heavier at the stern. In a headwind, shift a little weight forward; in a tailwind, a little back.
- **Nothing above the gunwales.** Packs stacked high make the boat tippy and catch the wind.
- **Stay under capacity.** The Northline 17 Kevlar is rated for 900 lb, the Northline 17 Aluminum for 850 lb, the Wayfarer 18.5 for 1,150 lb, and the Kestrel 15 for 425 lb. Those numbers include paddlers. On open water, you want plenty of margin.
- **Clip or tie in loose items.** Water bottles, maps, and the spare paddle should be secured so they don't float off if you tip.

## End of the day

### Store it hull-up

At camp, carry the canoe well above the waterline and turn it hull-up. Hull-up storage keeps rain out, keeps the seats dry, and keeps the boat from being picked up by wind. Tie it to a tree with the 50-ft rope that comes with your boat. Wind can flip and move an untied canoe, even a heavy one. Put the paddles and PFDs underneath it.

### Check it over

Before you turn in, take a minute to look over the hull. Run a hand along the bottom for cracks or soft spots and check the seats, yoke, and thwarts. If you find a crack in a Kevlar hull, keep the boat out of rough water and plan an easier route out. A small crack can grow under stress.

## Rinse it before return

Before you bring the canoe back, rinse out the sand, grit, and mud. Grit trapped under packs acts like sandpaper on the inside of the hull, and it makes checking the boat at return slow. A bucket or two of lake water swept out on your last morning is enough. Then carry, don't drag, the canoe up our landing at Ashby Lake and set it on the rack hull-up.

## Quick checklist

- [ ] Unload and load only while the canoe is floating
- [ ] Lift the canoe clear at every landing; never drag it

- [ ] Canoe stored hull-up and tied off every night
